Kids Coding Guide (Age 6)

A calm, play-first way to introduce a six-year-old to computers and coding. Start with clicks and puzzles, move to screen-free logic, then let them snap blocks together to make a story come alive.

Core approach

Play first. Think visually. Make stories.

At six, coding is less about syntax and more about noticing patterns, giving clear instructions, and fixing things when they do not go to plan. Keep activities short, playful, and full of immediate feedback.

Play-based learning

Turn each idea into a game: a treasure hunt, a robot walk, a silly story. If it feels like play, the child will keep coming back.

Visual logic

Use colours, arrows, blocks, and pictures instead of typed code. The child can see the order of steps and change one piece at a time.

Create & tell stories

Let the child choose the character, the goal, and the ending. A story gives the coding a purpose and makes the result worth sharing.

Coach the thinking, not the answer.

Instead of “That’s wrong,” ask: “What did the computer do? What could we change?” That turns every mistake into a tiny debugging lesson.

Step 1

Basic computer familiarity

Build comfort before code. Short, successful interactions matter more than speed.

Mouse & trackpad skills

Practice clicking, double-clicking, and dragging through simple, engaging mini-games.

  • Click: pop bubbles, match pairs, or feed a virtual pet.
  • Double-click: open a folder or launch a drawing app.
  • Drag and drop: sort shapes, build a puzzle, or move blocks.

Try this: “Can you put every red shape in the red box?”

Keyboard basics

Introduce the main keys by what they do, not by typing speed.

  • Letter keys: find the first letter of their name.
  • Spacebar: jump, pause, or add a space in a drawing app.
  • Enter: send a simple command or start a game.
  • Arrow keys: move a character one step at a time.

Try this: “Use the arrows to walk your character to the star.”

Healthy screen-time habits

Keep sessions short and finish while the child is still enjoying them.

  • Keep it short: around 20–30 minutes per session.
  • Look away: every 10–15 minutes, focus on something far away.
  • Sit comfortably: feet supported, screen at eye level, wrists relaxed.
  • Move between sessions: stretch, walk, or play a quick offline game.

Try this: set a visual timer and finish with a favourite offline activity.

Step 2

Unplugged coding logic

No screen needed. These games teach the ideas behind code using bodies, cards, and everyday routines.

Sequence

Play “human robot.” Give one instruction at a time and follow it exactly.

  • “Move forward two steps.”
  • “Turn left.”
  • “Move forward one step.”

If the robot bumps into a chair, that is a bug. Fixing the instructions together is debugging.

Try this: draw a simple obstacle course and guide each other through it.

Loops

Show repetition with actions the child already knows.

  • “Clap three times. Now repeat that three times.”
  • “Do four star jumps, then repeat the whole set twice.”

A loop is a shortcut for “do this again.”

Try this: build a short clap-and-stomp pattern, then loop it.

Conditions (if–then)

Introduce basic decision-making rules with everyday examples.

  • “If it rains, bring an umbrella; else, just walk.”
  • “If the light is red, stop; else, go.”
  • “If you are hungry, choose a snack; else, keep playing.”

Conditions help a program choose what to do next.

Try this: make a “morning robot” that chooses clothes based on the weather.

Human robot game

Parent: “Forward two steps. Turn right. Forward one step.”

Child: follows the instructions exactly, then checks the goal.

Together: “What happened? Which instruction should we change?”

Step 3

Block-based coding (visual programming)

Visual blocks remove the typing barrier. The child snaps instructions together and sees the result immediately.

Snap blocks together

Start with one or two blocks: move, jump, play a sound. Add a new block only when the first one works.

  • Watch the character move.
  • Listen for the sound.
  • Change one block and try again.

Try this: make a character take three steps, then play a sound.

Build a story or mini-game

Let the child choose the theme: a cat chasing a star, a rocket landing, a dinosaur dance party.

  • Who is the main character?
  • What happens first?
  • How does the story end?

Try this: plan the story on paper before opening the app.

Solve it together

Stay curious and collaborative. Ask questions instead of grabbing the mouse.

  • “Why did that happen?”
  • “Which block should we adjust?”
  • “What should we try next?”

Try this: let the child explain the bug before you offer a fix.

Keep the session small.

One new idea per session is plenty. Finish while it still feels fun, and leave the next step for another day.

A simple rhythm

A four-week starting plan

Repeat or reorder as you like. Short, happy sessions beat long, tired ones.

  1. Week 1

    Explore the computer

    Click, double-click, drag, and find the main keys. Play one mouse game.

  2. Week 2

    Play human robot

    Practice sequence, loops, and if-then rules with bodies and everyday objects.

  3. Week 3

    Try ScratchJr

    Make a character move, add a sound, and change one block at a time.

  4. Week 4

    Build a tiny story

    Choose a character, a goal, and an ending. Share it with someone.
